\begin{abstract}
The IAPWS-IF97 formulation is the international standard for water and steam thermodynamic properties, 
but direct formula evaluation cannot meet the compute-intensive demands of large-scale simulations requiring millions of
 property calculations. Existing approaches to improve computational speed fall into two categories: 
 (1) direct formula evaluation with fast integer power functions (repeated squaring), which provides limited acceleration; and
  (2) alternative methods such as TTSE and SBTL that avoid direct formula evaluation but sacrifice accuracy and support only limited 
  input parameter pairs. This paper presents two acceleration techniques that directly optimize the IAPWS-IF97 formula evaluation:
   loop tiling and scaling for derivatively related polynomial computation. 
   Loop tiling partitions polynomial summation into cache-friendly tiles  that enable effective SIMD vectorization. 
   Scaling for derivatively related polynomial computation exploits the mathematical relationship between Gibbs/Helmholtz free energy polynomials and their partial derivatives to compute them simultaneously in a single pass, eliminating redundant calculations. These techniques maintain full formula accuracy while supporting all 12 input parameter pairs. A Rust-based software package, SEUIF97, implements these methods and computes 36 thermodynamic, transport, and derived properties. Benchmark results demonstrate 5x to 20x speedups compared to baseline implementations using fast integer power functions with a single for loop, with the largest improvements in Regions 1, 2, and 3 where polynomial forms are most complex.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The IAPWS-IF97 formulation \cite{IAPWS-IF97} is the international standard for calculating thermodynamic properties of water and steam. Since its release, it has become the de facto standard in power engineering, process simulation, and thermal system analysis \cite{Wagner2008}. High-performance computing scenarios such as real-time simulation, optimization, and large-scale model training require fast and accurate water/steam property calculations to meet compute-intensive demands.

The core computational work of IF97 consists of evaluatiing Gibbs and Helmholtz free energy polynomials and their partial derivatives, which can become a bottleneck in large-scale simulations requiring millions of property calculations. Since IF97 equations are based on polynomials with integer exponents and their partial derivatives, achieving fast property calculations requires designing efficient evaluation methods for these polynomials. This creates a critical need for optimization techniques that can accelerate such polynomial computations without sacrificing accuracy.

Existing approaches to improving IAPWS-IF97 computational speed fall into two categories. The first category directly evaluates the IF97 formulas with limited optimization. Among open-source implementations, freesteam \cite{FreeSteam} and CoolProp/IF97 \cite{CoolProp,CoolPropIF97} have applied the repeated squaring method in their C implementations to optimize integer power computation. Most other implementations, including widely used libraries, directly evaluate the formulas term by term without exploiting the polynomial structure for acceleration. The second category uses alternative formulations that avoid direct polynomial evaluation. IAPWS has proposed the TTSE (Tabular Taylor Series Expansion) \cite{TTSE} and SBTL (Spline-Based Table Lookup) \cite{SBTL} methods, which use precomputed tables with interpolation to achieve fast lookups. However, these methods do not directly evaluate the IF97 formulas, have limited applicability to specific input parameter pairs, and require additional memory for table storage.

Despite these efforts, significant opportunities for optimization remain unexplored: (1) the recurrence relationship between polynomial terms and their derivatives has not been exploited to eliminate redundant calculations; (2) loop tiling for improving cache locality in polynomial evaluation has not been applied in the IF97 context; and (3) no existing implementation systematically combines multiple optimization techniques while maintaining full formula accuracy and support for all input parameter pairs.

This paper proposes two acceleration techniques that directly optimize the IAPWS-IF97 formula evaluation:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Loop tiling}: Splitting single loops into cache-friendly tiles to improve cache locality and SIMD vectorization
    \item \textbf{Scaling for derivatively related polynomial computation}: Computing polynomials and their partial derivatives simultaneously in a single pass to eliminate redundant calculations
\end{enumerate}

The paper is organized as follows: Section 2 provides an overview of the IAPWS-IF97 formulation and its key characteristics; Section 3 details the acceleration techniques; Section 4 presents comprehensive benchmark results; Section 5 describes the software implementation; and Section 6 concludes the paper.

\subsection{Basic Equations}

The core of IAPWS-IF97 consists of two types of fundamental equations, each optimized for specific thermodynamic regions (Regions 1, 2, 3, and 5). Region 4 (saturation line) provides explicit equations for saturation pressure $p_s(T)$ and saturation temperature $T_s(p)$.

\subsubsection{Gibbs Free Energy Equation (Regions 1, 2, 5)}

Regions 1 (compressed liquid), 2 (superheated vapor), and 5 (high-temperature steam) use the dimensionless Gibbs free energy $g/(RT)$ as the fundamental equation. For Regions 2 and 5, the dimensionless Gibbs free energy is split into an ideal-gas part $\gamma^0$ and a residual part $\gamma^r$:

\begin{equation}
\frac{g(p,T)}{RT} = \gamma(\pi,\tau) = \gamma^0(\pi,\tau) + \gamma^r(\pi,\tau)
\label{eq:gibbs}
\end{equation}

where the reduced variables are defined as $\pi = p/p^{*}$ and $\tau = T^{*}/T$, with region-specific scaling parameters $p^{*}$ and $T^{*}$. The ideal-gas part $\gamma^0$ has the form:

\begin{equation}
\gamma^0(\pi,\tau) = \ln\pi + \sum_{i=1}^{N_0} n_i^0 \, \tau^{J_i^0}
\label{eq:gibbs_ideal}
\end{equation}

The residual part $\gamma^r$ is a polynomial in $\pi$ and $\tau$:

\begin{equation}
\gamma^r(\pi,\tau) = \sum_{i=1}^{N_r} n_i \, \pi^{I_i} \, \tau^{J_i}
\label{eq:gibbs_residual}
\end{equation}

For Region 1, the fundamental equation uses a single polynomial with shifted variables:

\begin{equation}
\gamma(\pi,\tau) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} n_i \, (7.1 - \pi)^{I_i} \, (\tau - 1.222)^{J_i}
\label{eq:gibbs_r1}
\end{equation}

where $\pi = p/p^{*}$ with $p^{*} = 16.53$ MPa and $\tau = T^{*}/T$ with $T^{*} = 1386$ K. The coefficients $n_i$ and exponents $I_i$, $J_i$ are provided in tabular form for each region. Table \ref{tab:region_params} summarizes the scaling parameters and number of terms for each region.

\subsubsection{Helmholtz Free Energy Equation (Region 3)}

Region 3 (near-critical region) uses the dimensionless Helmholtz free energy $f/(RT)$ as the fundamental equation, with density $\rho$ and temperature $T$ as independent variables:

\begin{equation}
\frac{f(\rho,T)}{RT} = \phi(\delta,\tau) = n_1 \ln\delta + \sum_{i=1}^{N} n_i \, \delta^{I_i} \, \tau^{J_i}
\label{eq:helmholtz}
\end{equation}

where $\delta = \rho/\rho^{*}$ and $\tau = T^{*}/T$, with $\rho^{*} = 322$ kg/m$^3$ and $T^{*} = 647.096$ K. The leading $n_1 \ln\delta$ term captures the ideal-gas behavior, and the 39 polynomial terms model the residual contribution.


\subsection{Thermodynamic Property Derivation}

All thermodynamic properties are derived from the fundamental equations and their partial derivatives. In Regions 1, 2, and 5, properties are derived from the Gibbs free energy $\gamma(\pi,\tau)$, while in Region 3, properties are derived from the Helmholtz free energy $\phi(\delta,\tau)$. Although the specific expressions differ by region, all share the same computational pattern: evaluating polynomial summations and their partial derivatives. Taking Region 2 as a representative example, the key properties derived from $\gamma(\pi,\tau) = \gamma^0(\pi,\tau) + \gamma^r(\pi,\tau)$ are:

\begin{align}
h &= RT \cdot \tau \left( \frac{\partial \gamma}{\partial \tau} \right)_\pi
    = RT \cdot \tau \left( \gamma^0_\tau + \gamma^r_\tau \right) \\
s &= R \left[ \tau \left( \frac{\partial \gamma}{\partial \tau} \right)_\pi - \gamma \right]
    = R \left[ \tau \left( \gamma^0_\tau + \gamma^r_\tau \right) - \left( \gamma^0 + \gamma^r \right) \right] \label{eq:entropy} \\
w^2 &= RT \cdot \frac{1 + 2\pi\gamma^r_\pi + \pi^2(\gamma^r_\pi)^2}{\left( 1 - \pi^2\gamma^r_{\pi\pi} \right) + \dfrac{\left( 1 + \pi\gamma^r_\pi - \tau\pi\gamma^r_{\pi\tau} \right)^2}{\tau^2\left( \gamma^0_{\tau\tau} + \gamma^r_{\tau\tau} \right)}} \label{eq:speed}
\end{align}

where the partial derivatives of the ideal-gas part $\gamma^0$ and the residual part $\gamma^r$ are:

\begin{align}
\gamma^0_\tau &= \sum_{i=1}^{N_0} n_i^0 J_i^0 \tau^{J_i^0 - 1}, &
\gamma^0_{\tau\tau} &= \sum_{i=1}^{N_0} n_i^0 J_i^0 (J_i^0 - 1) \tau^{J_i^0 - 2} \\
\gamma^r_\pi &= \sum_{i=1}^{N_r} n_i I_i \pi^{I_i - 1} \tau^{J_i}, &
\gamma^r_{\pi\pi} &= \sum_{i=1}^{N_r} n_i I_i (I_i - 1) \pi^{I_i - 2} \tau^{J_i} \\
\gamma^r_\tau &= \sum_{i=1}^{N_r} n_i J_i \pi^{I_i} \tau^{J_i - 1}, &
\gamma^r_{\tau\tau} &= \sum_{i=1}^{N_r} n_i J_i (J_i - 1) \pi^{I_i} \tau^{J_i - 2} \\
\gamma^r_{\pi\tau} &= \sum_{i=1}^{N_r} n_i I_i J_i \pi^{I_i - 1} \tau^{J_i - 1}
\end{align}

Other properties such as specific volume $v = \frac{RT}{p^*}(\gamma^0_\pi + \gamma^r_\pi)$ and specific isobaric heat capacity $c_p = -R\tau^2(\gamma^0_{\tau\tau} + \gamma^r_{\tau\tau})$ follow the same pattern. In Region 3, the analogous properties are derived from $\phi(\delta,\tau)$ with partial derivatives with respect to $\delta$ and $\tau$. Despite these differences, the core computation in all regions reduces to evaluating polynomial summations and their partial derivatives of various orders.

\subsection{Formula Characteristics and Computational Implications}

The IAPWS-IF97 formulation exhibits several key characteristics that directly impact computational performance:

\textbf{Characteristic 1: Polynomial structure with integer exponents.} The residual parts of all fundamental equations are sums of terms of the form $n_i x^{I_i} y^{J_i}$, where $I_i$ and $J_i$ are integers and $(x, y)$ are the reduced variables for each region. This structure allows efficient evaluation using integer power operations rather than general exponentiation.

\textbf{Characteristic 2: Derivatives share the same polynomial form.} The partial derivatives of the fundamental equations are themselves polynomials with the same base terms, only with modified coefficients. For example, comparing Eq.~(\ref{eq:gibbs_residual}) with the derivative $\gamma^r_\tau$:

\begin{align}
\gamma^r &= \sum_{i=1}^{N_r} n_i \, \pi^{I_i} \, \tau^{J_i} \\
\left( \frac{\partial \gamma^r}{\partial \tau} \right)_\pi &= \sum_{i=1}^{N_r} (n_i J_i) \, \pi^{I_i} \, \tau^{J_i - 1} =\frac{1}{\tau} \sum_{i=1}^{N_r} (n_i J_i) \, \pi^{I_i} \, \tau^{J_i}
\end{align}

This means that computing multiple thermodynamic properties requires evaluating several polynomials with the same base terms $\pi^{I_i} \tau^{J_i}$ but different coefficient multipliers.

\textbf{Characteristic 3: High term counts in main regions.} Regions 1 and 2, which cover the most commonly used thermodynamic conditions in engineering applications, contain 34 and 52 terms respectively. Each property calculation requires summing all these terms, making polynomial evaluation the dominant computational cost.

These characteristics motivate the acceleration techniques presented in this paper. The shared polynomial structure (Characteristic 2) enables recurrence polynomial-derivative computation when multiple related derivatives are needed, while the high term counts (Characteristic 3) benefit from loop tiling for all polynomial evaluations.

\section{Acceleration Techniques}

\subsection{Loop Tiling}

Loop tiling (also known as loop blocking) \cite{LoopTiling,Grosser2012Polly} is a general optimization technique that partitions a single loop into multiple smaller loops (tiles) to improve cache locality and enable effective SIMD vectorization. For IF97 polynomial evaluation, this technique applies to any property computation that involves a single polynomial summation.

Taking specific enthalpy $h$ in Region 2 as an example, the computation requires only the first-order partial derivative $\gamma_\tau$:

\begin{equation}
h = RT \cdot \tau \left( \gamma^0_\tau + \gamma^r_\tau \right)
\end{equation}

Each summation is a simple polynomial evaluation with a single accumulator. The loop tiling technique partitions the term indices into tiles that fit within CPU cache, reducing memory access latency and allowing the compiler to optimize each tile independently.

Algorithm \ref{alg:loop_tiling} presents the loop tiling approach for a single polynomial summation:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Loop Tiling for Single Polynomial Summation}
\label{alg:loop_tiling}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $x, y$: reduced variables (e.g., $\pi, \tau$ for Regions 2/5; $7.1{-}\pi, \tau{-}1.222$ for Region 1; $\delta, \tau$ for Region 3)
\REQUIRE $IJn$: array of $(I_i, J_i, n_i)$ polynomial coefficients
\REQUIRE $\text{steps}$: array of $(start, end)$ tile boundaries
\ENSURE $S = \sum_i n_i x^{I_i} y^{J_i}$: polynomial summation result

\STATE Initialize $S \leftarrow 0$
\FOR{each tile $m$ in $\text{steps}$}
    \FOR{$k \leftarrow \text{steps}[m].start$ \TO $\text{steps}[m].end - 1$}
        \STATE $term \leftarrow IJn[k].n \times x^{IJn[k].I} \times y^{IJn[k].J}$
        \STATE $S \leftarrow S + term$
    \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $S$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The optimal tile boundaries vary across regions and polynomial types, and are determined empirically through performance profiling to identify the partitioning that maximizes compiler optimization potential. For Region 2 (43 residual terms), we use three tiles: 0--13, 13--26, and 26--43. This partitioning allows the Rust compiler to optimize each tile independently through loop unrolling, SIMD vectorization, and register allocation. The same loop tiling strategy applies to all single-polynomial computations, including $h$, $c_p$, $v$, and individual derivative evaluations.

Performance profiling demonstrates the effectiveness of this approach. All measurements were conducted on an Intel Core i7-13700K processor (24 cores, up to 5.4 GHz) with 32 GB DDR5 RAM, running Windows 11, using Rust 1.75.0 compiled in release mode with \texttt{opt-level = 3} and \texttt{target-cpu = native}. For Region 2 enthalpy computation ($h$), loop tiling reduces the execution time from approximately 1.07 µs to 53.8 ns, achieving a ~20x speedup. This improvement stems from better cache utilization and the compiler's ability to apply aggressive optimizations to each tile independently.

\subsection{Scaling for Derivatively Related Polynomial computation}

When a thermodynamic property requires multiple polynomial evaluations that share the same base terms, scaling for derivatively related polynomial computation eliminates redundant calculations by computing them simultaneously in a single pass.

Taking specific entropy $s$ in Region 2 as an example, the computation requires both $\gamma$ and $\gamma_\tau$:

\begin{equation}
s = R \left[ \tau \left( \gamma^0_\tau + \gamma^r_\tau \right) - \left( \gamma^0 + \gamma^r \right) \right]
\end{equation}

The key observation is that $\gamma^r$ and its partial derivatives with respect to $\tau$ share the same base term $n_i \pi^{I_i} \tau^{J_i}$ and differ only in their coefficient multipliers:

\begin{align}
\gamma^0_\tau &= \sum_{i=1}^{N_0} n_i^0 J_i^0 \tau^{J_i^0 - 1} \\
\gamma^r_\tau &= \sum_{i=1}^{N_r} (n_i J_i) \, \pi^{I_i} \, \tau^{J_i-1} = \frac{1}{\tau} \sum_{i=1}^{N_r} (n_i J_i) \, \pi^{I_i} \, \tau^{J_i}
\end{align}

By accumulating these coefficient-weighted sums in parallel within the same loop, we eliminate the redundant recomputation of the base terms $\pi^{I_i} \tau^{J_i}$.

Algorithm \ref{alg:recurrence} presents the recurrence polynomial-derivative computation combined with loop tiling for the common case of computing the polynomial value and its two first-order partial derivatives simultaneously:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Scaling for Derivatively Related Polynomial Computation with Loop Tiling}
\label{alg:recurrence}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $x, y$: reduced variables (e.g., $\pi, \tau$ for Regions 2/5; $7.1{-}\pi, \tau{-}1.222$ for Region 1; $\delta, \tau$ for Region 3)
\REQUIRE $IJn$: array of $(I_i, J_i, n_i)$ polynomial coefficients
\REQUIRE $\text{steps}$: array of $(start, end)$ tile boundaries
\ENSURE $\gamma, \partial\gamma/\partial x, \partial\gamma/\partial y$: polynomial and its partial derivatives

\STATE Initialize $\gamma \leftarrow 0$, $\gamma_x \leftarrow 0$, $\gamma_y \leftarrow 0$
\FOR{each tile $m$ in $\text{steps}$}
    \FOR{$k \leftarrow \text{steps}[m].start$ \TO $\text{steps}[m].end - 1$}
        \STATE $term \leftarrow IJn[k].n \times x^{IJn[k].I} \times y^{IJn[k].J}$
        \STATE $\gamma \leftarrow \gamma + term$
        \STATE $\gamma_x \leftarrow \gamma_x + IJn[k].I \times term$
        \STATE $\gamma_y \leftarrow \gamma_y + IJn[k].J \times term$
    \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\STATE $\gamma_x \leftarrow \gamma_x / x$
\STATE $\gamma_y \leftarrow \gamma_y / y$
\RETURN $(\gamma, \gamma_x, \gamma_y)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm \ref{alg:recurrence} shows the general case of computing one polynomial value and two first-order partial derivatives simultaneously. For properties requiring only one derivative (e.g., entropy $s$ needs $\gamma$ and $\gamma_\tau$), the same pattern applies with only the relevant derivative accumulator active. For properties requiring higher-order or mixed derivatives (e.g., speed of sound $w$), additional accumulators are added following the same coefficient-multiplication principle.

Performance profiling under the same hardware and software environment as 
Section 3.1 demonstrates the effectiveness of this approach. 
For Region 2 entropy computation ($s$), which requires both $\gamma$ and $\gamma_\tau$,
the scaling for derivatively related polynomial computation combined with loop tiling reduces the execution time from approximately 2.2 µs to 138.7 ns, achieving a ~15x speedup. This confirms that computing multiple related derivatives simultaneously eliminates redundant base term evaluations while maintaining the cache benefits of loop tiling.

\subsection{Combined Application}

The two techniques are applied based on the computational requirements of each property:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Loop tiling only}: Applied to properties requiring a single polynomial summation, such as $h = RT\tau\gamma_\tau$ and $c_p = -R\tau^2\gamma_{\tau\tau}$. Each derivative is computed independently with its own tiled loop.
    \item \textbf{Scaling + Loop tiling}: Applied to properties requiring multiple related polynomials, such as $s = R[\tau\gamma_\tau - \gamma]$ and $w$ (which requires $\gamma_\pi$, $\gamma_{\pi\pi}$, $\gamma_{\pi\tau}$, $\gamma_{\tau\tau}$). The recurrence technique computes all required derivatives simultaneously, and loop tiling is applied to the combined loop.
\end{itemize}

This selective application ensures that the overhead of recurrence computation is only incurred when it provides a net benefit, while loop tiling is universally applied to all polynomial summations.

\section{Software Implementation: SEUIF97}

Based on the acceleration techniques presented in Section 3, SEUIF97 is implemented in Rust as a high-performance IAPWS-IF97 property calculation library. The library follows a modular three-layer architecture:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Public API}: User-facing functions supporting multiple input parameter pairs (p-T, p-h, p-s, etc.)
    \item \textbf{Region wrapper}: Region-specific functions handling coordinate transformations and step boundary definitions
    \item \textbf{Optimized kernel}: The core \texttt{polys\_i\_j\_powi\_steps} function implementing loop tiling and recurrence polynomial-derivative computation
\end{enumerate}

To meet the needs of comprehensive water and steam property calculations across diverse engineering applications, SEUIF97 supports 12 input parameter pairs: $(p,t)$, $(p,h)$, $(p,s)$, $(p,v)$, $(t,h)$, $(t,s)$, $(t,v)$, $(h,s)$, $(p,x)$, $(t,x)$, $(h,x)$, $(s,x)$, and computes 36 thermodynamic, transport, and derived properties, including basic properties ($p$, $t$, $\rho$, $v$, $h$, $s$, $u$), derived properties ($c_p$, $c_v$, $w$, Helmholtz/Gibbs free energy, compressibility factor), partial derivatives, and transport properties ($\mu$, $\lambda$, $\sigma$).

\section{Conclusions}

This paper proposed two acceleration techniques for the IAPWS-IF97 formula evaluation: loop tiling and
scaling for derivatively related polynomial computation. The key insight is that the IAPWS-IF97 fundamental equations and their partial derivatives share the same polynomial base terms, enabling simultaneous computation in a single pass. Combined with loop tiling for cache optimization, the proposed approach achieves 5x to 20x speedups over baseline implementations that use fast integer power functions with a single for loop, with the largest improvements in Regions 1, 2 and 3 where the polynomial forms are most complex. These results demonstrate that directly accelerating the IAPWS-IF97 formulas can achieve competitive performance without resorting to approximation-based methods such as TTSE and SBTL. The Rust-based software package SEUIF97, available in multiple package formats, provides convenience for developers working in different programming environments.
